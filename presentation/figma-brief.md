# Figma Deck Brief — Stream 1: Credit Card Default

This is a **content and evidence brief**, not a layout spec. `presentation/slides.html` is a separate, already-finished execution of this same story — don't open it for layout reference. The point of building this again in Figma is to get an independent, original visual take that uses what Figma is actually good at (real typography controls, component variants, effects, motion prototyping) rather than tracing a browser page. Treat the HTML deck as "the content has been proven to work," not as a template.

## The story arc (10 beats — feel free to re-split, merge, or reorder these)

1. **The problem.** Predict P(default) for a credit-card customer, scored by log loss. Scale: 24,000 customers, 23 raw columns, 22.121% base default rate, 6,000 hidden test customers. Final result: **0.40982** hidden-test log loss (1st place: 0.40967).
2. **Data understanding.** Zero missing values, but undocumented category codes discovered by inspecting unique values per column: `EDUCATION` codes {0,5,6} → 4 ("other", 290 rows), `MARRIAGE` code {0} → 3 ("other", 42 rows). Separately: `PAY_*` values of -1/-2 are *informative* states (paid in full / no balance), not missing data — kept as features, never imputed.
3. **The one feature that mattered.** The data never states how much a customer *spent* in a month — only balances and payments. That quantity is recoverable exactly via an accounting identity: `spend_t = BILL_t − BILL_(t+1) + PAY_AMT_t`. This single feature family was worth **−0.00173** on hidden test — the largest gain of the whole project. Six other engineered feature families were tried and failed (behavioural signatures +0.00006, spend-as-sequence +0.00002, interaction terms 0.00000, peer-relative limit −0.00011, velocity/acceleration −0.00024, target encoding −0.00052).
4. **The experimentation arc.** 41 logged experiments, only 4 produced gains surviving a 0.0005 noise floor. Progression across 8 submissions: 0.41260 → 0.41237 → 0.41211 → 0.41211 → **0.41038** (spend decomposition, the big jump) → 0.41032 → 0.40995 → **0.40982** (final). One honesty beat worth dramatizing: isotonic calibration looked like the best result on record until a leakage bug was found — corrected, it became the single *worst* result (−0.00330).
5. **How a prediction is actually made (the mechanism beat — don't skip this one).** Follow one real validation customer — late the last two months, 100% credit utilisation, paid under 5% of a recent bill — through all three components: **LightGBM** starts at the 22.1% base rate and adds ~352 shallow decision-tree corrections in sequence (its first split is always some version of "were they recently late?"), landing at **62.7%**. **The GRU** reads the same six months in order, updating a 32-value memory forwards and backwards, and reaches **34.3%** — it separates a recovering customer from a deteriorating one even when their aggregate counts are identical, which the flat LightGBM view cannot. **TabPFN** is shown 19,200 labelled customers as live context and returns **31.2%** in a single forward pass, with zero parameters fit to this data. Blended (0.45/0.30/0.25): **46.3%** predicted. That customer defaulted. This is the single best piece of evidence for "why three models" — it's not abstract, it's one customer scored three genuinely different ways.
6. **Model selection & validation (the numbers behind beat 5).** Three models blended because they read a customer three different ways, not because they're different algorithms on the same view: **LightGBM** (45% weight) reads 81 engineered features as flat statistics, OOF 0.42211; **GRU** (30%) reads the same 6 months as an ordered trajectory, OOF 0.42392; **TabPFN** (25%) applies a pretrained prior with zero parameters fit to this data, OOF 0.42345. Weights were chosen by grid search on out-of-fold log loss, not guessed. Validation: stratified 5-fold CV repeated over 6 fold partitions, noise floor 0.0005. Final: OOF 0.42136 → hidden test 0.40982. This beat can be compact (a weights table + validation numbers) precisely because beat 5 already did the storytelling work.
7. **What drives risk (insight).** SHAP attribution: top predictors are `months_since_late`, `PAY_0`, `recent_late` — recency of lateness beats raw balance/utilization. One real customer example at each extreme: 84.9% predicted risk (driven by recent lateness) vs. 2.2% (six clean months, high available credit). The existing SHAP summary chart (`output/analysis/shap_summary.png`) is available to reuse as raw reference, but redraw it in Figma's own visual language rather than importing the matplotlib image if you want full consistency with the rest of the deck.
8. **Where the model fails (the honest slide).** Two distinct, concrete failure modes: (a) `EDUCATION="other"` subgroup (n=387) — actual default rate 7.24%, mean predicted 11.62% (over-predicted 61% relative), AUC only 0.645 vs. ~0.79 for every other education group; this is traced directly to the cleaning decision in beat 2. (b) The 20 worst-predicted customers are all real defaulters scored at 2–4% risk, every one with a spotless payment history — a ceiling the current data cannot see past.
9. **From model to decision (the stream's "plan a course of action" ask).** False negative = credit extended that becomes bad debt (direct $ loss). False positive = a good customer declined (revenue loss + a fairness cost, sharpened by beat 8a). Recommended action: a three-tier policy — auto-approve low risk / human review for the middle band **and** any subgroup known to be miscalibrated / auto-decline only the extreme high-risk tail. Explicitly do not auto-decline the "other" education segment given its poor calibration.
10. **Limitations & what's next.** LightGBM alone reaches 0.42211 OOF; the full 3-model blend reaches 0.42136 — a 0.0007 gain for 180 extra model fits and a TabPFN dependency that's licence-gated and takes ~3h10m per run. Blend-weight selection is the one step in the pipeline that could theoretically overfit (everything else — averaging, repeated CV, refits — structurally can't). Closing line: a better result needs a new insight about the data, not more tuning.

## What each beat needs to *do* visually (not how to lay it out)

- Beats 1, 6, 10 are **headline-number moments** — a single statistic should dominate and be instantly legible from the back of a room.
- Beats 3, 5, 7 are **"aha" moments** — something counterintuitive or clever (an accounting identity recovering hidden information; one customer scored three genuinely different ways; recency beating magnitude) deserves a beat of visual emphasis or reveal, not just a bullet. Beat 5 especially: this is a worked walkthrough, not a summary — it can afford a slower reveal (three components appearing in sequence, then converging on the blended number) since it's the deck's clearest proof of "why an ensemble."
- Beat 4 is a **narrative-over-time moment** — this is the one place a timeline/progression device earns its keep, because it's a real sequence of decisions in order, not a list.
- Beats 2, 8 are **evidence moments** — judges are told to reward "support your conclusions with evidence," so these should feel like proof (real numbers, real subgroup counts), not vibes.
- Beat 9 is a **decision/recommendation moment** — this is the one slide making a forward-looking claim rather than reporting a result; it can carry a different visual register (more decisive, less analytical) than the rest of the deck.

## Design direction (a starting point, not a mandate — make it your own)

- **Domain-grounded, not generic-corporate.** This is credit-statement data — real texture exists in the domain itself (ledgers, statements, tabular figures, month-over-month tracking) that a generic "startup pitch deck" look would throw away. Consider leaning into that materiality (tabular/monospaced numerals for all figures, a statement- or spreadsheet-adjacent grid logic) without literally copying a bank statement.
- **One accent color used with intent**, not a rainbow of category colors — most of this content is sequential/evidentiary, not many-category comparison, so a restrained palette (one neutral ink + one accent + reserved status colors for the two or three moments that are genuinely "good/bad/warning," like beat 8 and the tier policy in beat 9) will read as more confident than a colorful deck.
- **Typography can carry more personality here than the HTML version did** — Figma's type tooling (optical sizing, real tracking control, variable fonts) is worth using deliberately rather than defaulting to a generic sans; pick a pairing that feels intentional for a data-driven, evidentiary story rather than a template default.
- **Use Figma-native strengths the HTML deck couldn't lean on as easily**: component variants for repeating a "stat card" or "evidence card" pattern consistently across beats, auto-layout so spacing stays systematic as content changes, and — if presenting live from Figma or exporting a walkthrough — prototyped transitions between beats 3→4→5→6 (the causal chain: one feature → one experimentation arc → one customer walked through the mechanism → the weights that resulted) to make that throughline felt rather than stated.
- **Numbers are the evidence — protect their legibility.** Every number cited above should be traceable back to this brief or the source docs (`REPORT.md`, `output/analysis/fairness_audit.txt`, `docs/experiment-ledger.html`) — don't round differently than what's written here, and don't invent chart data that isn't backed by a real number in those files.
