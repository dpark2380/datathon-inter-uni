What the model is, and how it came to be that

Approach

The task is to predict the probability that a customer defaults on their next payment, scored on binary log loss over 6,000 hidden customers. Because that metric rewards well-calibrated probabilities rather than correct labels, every decision was judged on log loss directly — never on accuracy, and never on AUC.

Two consequences followed from the metric and shaped everything below. We never rebalanced the classes, because the 22.121% positive rate is the rate the test set is drawn from and log loss rewards predicting it honestly. And we optimised the loss the metric actually uses at every stage: the GRU trains on binary cross-entropy, LightGBM on binary log loss, and the blend weights are chosen on log loss rather than on any proxy.

Every customer then passes through the same five stages. The only branch is at modelling, where three learners see the same rows through different lenses.

stage 1CleanUndocumented category codes collapsed: EDUCATION 0/5/6 → 4, MARRIAGE 0 → 3.
stage 2Engineer23 raw columns → 81 features: lateness history, utilisation, payment coverage, spending decomposition.
stage 3ModelLightGBM, GRU and TabPFN each trained on identical folds so predictions align row-for-row.
stage 4BlendFixed weighted average of probabilities, weights chosen by out-of-fold log loss.
stage 5EmitClip to [1e-4, 1−1e-4] and write client_id, prob_default. No other post-processing.

Where the split sits. The train/test division is fixed by the competition, and no step before modelling ever consults a label. Cleaning and feature engineering are pure row-wise functions applied identically to both sets, so they can safely run before the fold split. Everything that is fitted — the neural network’s scalers, blend weights, every calibrator we tested — is fitted on training folds only and scored on rows it never saw.

Data cleaning and preprocessing

Cleaning is deliberately minimal. The dataset has no missing values and no malformed rows; the only real defect is undocumented category codes. Each of the three changes below collapses codes that carry the same meaning.

ColumnChangeReason
MARRIAGE code 0 → 3 The dictionary defines 1–3 only. An unknown marital status is not distinguishable from “other” for prediction purposes.
EDUCATION codes 0, 5, 6 → 4 The dictionary defines 1–4 only; 4 is already “other”. The three undocumented codes cover a small tail and behave like it.

Where the repayment codes are handled. PAY_0…PAY_6 carry three codes that all mean “not behind this month”: −2 (no balance), −1 (paid in full) and 0 (paid the minimum). We deliberately do not collapse them here. The raw columns are left intact, and the collapsing happens inside feature engineering instead — the lateness family is computed on PAY.clip(lower=0), which folds −2 and −1 into 0, while ever_paid_full and never_used read the original −1 and −2 codes. Collapsing at the cleaning stage would have destroyed those two features; doing it per-family keeps both readings available.

Implemented in common.clean() and applied identically to train and test. datasets/train_clean.csv is train.csv with exactly these two substitutions applied — 290 EDUCATION rows and 42 MARRIAGE rows — and is committed to the repository, regenerable by a single command. 2_Column_Inspection.ipynb is the notebook in which the substitutions were decided: it prints per-column unique values and null counts, which is how the undocumented codes were found.

Deliberately not done

Four common preprocessing steps were considered and rejected, each for a stated reason. Under log loss these are not neutral defaults — three of them would actively hurt.

  • No outlier removalLarge bills and payments are genuine, and log loss punishes mis-scoring them. The heavy-tailed amount columns are handled with log1p transforms inside feature engineering instead.
  • No resampling or class weightingThe 22.121% positive rate is the rate the test set is drawn from. Rebalancing shifts predicted probabilities off the true base rate, which is precisely what log loss penalises. This is also why TabPFN runs with balance_probabilities=False.
  • No imputation of the PAY_* sentinels−1 and −2 are informative states, not missing data. They are collapsed above because they share a meaning, and separately preserved as the flags ever_paid_full and never_used.
  • No scaling for the tree modelLightGBM is invariant to monotone transforms, so scaling it would change nothing. Scaling is applied only inside models/seq_model.py, and fitted on training folds only.

SEX, EDUCATION and MARRIAGE are kept as pandas category dtype so LightGBM handles them natively; the neural models one-hot encode them, and TabPFN receives them as integer codes with their column indices declared through categorical_features_indices.

Feature engineering

23 raw columns become 81 features in six families (common.features()). The design principle throughout is to convert absolute amounts into ratios that are comparable across customers with very different credit limits — and then, in one family, to deliberately put the scale back.

FamilyExamplesWhat it captures
Repayment history pay_max, pay_mean, n_late, n_late2plus, trend_pay, recent_late, max_late_streak, months_since_late, ever_paid_full, never_used How often, how badly and how recently the customer fell behind. recent_late weights lateness by how long ago it happened.
Credit utilisation util1…util6, util_mean, util_max, util_trend, avail_credit Balance as a fraction of the limit, and its direction. Consistently high utilisation suggests a customer short of funds.
Payment coverage payratio1…payratio5, payratio_mean, payratio_min, paid_full_months, n_zero_pay What fraction of each month’s bill was actually paid the following month.
Levels and momentum bill_sum/mean/std, amt_sum/mean/std, coverage_total, bill_growth, amt_over_limit, log_limit Absolute scale, log-compressed — the one family that puts back what the ratios strip out.
Spending decomposition spend1…spend5, spend_mean/max/std/trend/total, n_months_no_spend, spend_minus_paid, months_spend_gt_paid New information the dataset never states — see below.
Minimum-payment behaviour min_pay_ratio_mean, min_pay_ratio_min, months_paid_about_min, months_paid_under_min Whether the customer services only the minimum due (10% assumed) — a sign of strain, especially at high balances.

The spending decomposition — the largest single gain of the project. The dataset gives statement balances and payments made, but never the amount charged in a month. That quantity is recoverable exactly, from the identity that governs a revolving account:

BILL_t = BILL_(t+1) − PAY_AMT_t + spend_t   ⇒   spend_t = BILL_t − BILL_(t+1) + PAY_AMT_t

This matters because a rising balance is ambiguous without it. A balance rising because the customer is spending more is a different risk story from one rising because they stopped paying, and no combination of the pre-existing features could separate those two cases. Measured worth: +0.00046 out-of-fold and −0.00173 on the hidden test set — the largest move of the project.

Why one family deliberately restores scale

Every family except “levels and momentum” is a ratio or a count, which strips scale out on purpose — payratio3 is the same number whether the customer owes $500 or $50,000. That is usually what we want, but it discards something real: the absolute size of the debt, and whether it is growing.

Those features were added alongside the neural models, and the reason is a genuine difference between the two model types. A decision tree is scale-invariant: it splits on thresholds, so multiplying a feature by 1,000 or taking its logarithm changes nothing about which splits it finds. A neural network is not — it multiplies inputs by weights and sums them, so a feature measured in tens of thousands swamps one measured in fractions unless the inputs are standardised and the magnitudes are expressed in a usable form. These features give the network the scale information the ratio features removed.

The sequence view

For the GRU the same six months are additionally reshaped into a 6 timesteps × 8 channels panel, oldest month first: repayment status, a lateness flag, bill as a fraction of the limit, payment as a fraction of the limit, log-scaled bill and payment, the change in balance since the previous month, and the fraction of the previous bill the payment covered.

Six families that did not survive

Behavioural payment signatures (+0.00006), spending re-supplied as an ordered GRU channel (+0.00002), interaction terms (0.00000), peer-relative credit limit (−0.00011), velocity and acceleration derivatives (−0.00024) and target encoding (−0.00052) were all tested and none reached the noise floor.

The pattern is the point. The family that worked introduced a quantity the dataset never stated. The six that failed recombined or re-summarised information already present. This is the single most consistent finding of the whole experiment record, and it is why we stopped adding features rather than adding more.

Validation strategy

Stratified 5-fold cross-validation, repeated over 6 different fold partitions (common.folds, StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=seed) with seeds 0–5). Every model calls the same function with the same seeds, so all out-of-fold vectors are row-aligned and directly comparable.

Three properties of this design carried most of the weight — more than the choice of split itself.

  • A shared noise floorRepeating experiments on identical splits established that a difference below 0.0005 in out-of-fold log loss is not distinguishable from fold noise on 24,000 rows. Every result in the ledger is reported against that threshold, and no change below it was adopted on its point estimate alone.
  • Strictly out-of-fold scoring of anything fittedBlend weights, calibrators and target-derived features are all scored on rows the fitting never saw. This was not the original protocol — see the isotonic finding below.
  • A known, quantified blind spotRepeated CV and the full-data refit are both invisible to out-of-fold scoring by construction: each OOF row is predicted only by its own fold’s models, while every test row is predicted by the average of all of them, and OOF never involves a model refitted on 100% of the data. Confirmed on the leaderboard — the full-data refit is worth 0.00023 on the hidden test set and exactly zero on OOF.

The relationship between the two held steady throughout (OOF ≈ 0.4214 against hidden test ≈ 0.4098), so OOF decided whether to adopt a change and the leaderboard only confirmed it.

The mistake that set the protocol. Isotonic calibration initially looked like a large win — 0.41946 against a 0.42136 baseline. It was being fitted on the same out-of-fold predictions it was then scored against, and a flexible monotonic function will happily absorb the noise in the rows judging it. Re-run under proper nested cross-validation it scored 0.42508, the single worst result in our table. Every fitted combiner after that point was scored strictly out-of-fold. We record it because the corrected number, not the original one, is what the rest of this report is built on.

Models tested and final model selection

Ten model families were tried. Three earned blend weight. The blend works because these three disagree for reasons rather than at random — each is given the same customers and derives a different kind of evidence from them.

LightGBM

the aggregate view · weight 0.45 · OOF 0.42211 · AUC 0.79104

Gradient-boosted decision trees. Each tree looks at where the trees before it were wrong and fits those leftover errors; hundreds of shallow trees accumulate into one prediction. It reads the 81 engineered features as a flat description of the customer.

It is deliberately small. Depth 4 with 12 leaves and heavy L2 regularisation beat every deeper configuration across 135 tested: the signal is concentrated in recent repayment status, and larger trees memorise noise around it.

GRU

A recurrent network that walks the six months in order, oldest to newest, carrying a memory it updates at each step. Learned gates decide what to retain and what to discard, so it can weigh a recent lapse more heavily than an old one.

It exists because the aggregates are lossy in one specific way. Two customers each late twice — one recovering, one deteriorating — share an identical n_late, pay_max and pay_mean. The flat features literally cannot separate them; reading the months in sequence can.

Hyperparameter tuning

135 LightGBM configurations were tested across three sweeps: 40 coarse, 60 fine, and one scored by blend contribution rather than solo performance. The incumbent — originally tuned against AUC — was never beaten; the best challenger was 0.00004 better, twelve times below the noise floor. The GRU was the one model never previously tuned, and its architecture sweep produced a real gain (smaller hidden state, two layers, heavier dropout, faster learning rate), though it shipped in the same submission as the spending features and so is not independently isolated.

Why decorrelation was not the selection criterion. The intuition that an ensemble wants uncorrelated members is only half right, and this dataset produced an unusually clean counter-example. Logistic regression was the most decorrelated model in the entire project — 0.948 against LightGBM — and the weight search still gave it exactly 0.00. The GRU earned 0.30 at a higher correlation of 0.983. What matters is not that a model disagrees, but that its disagreements are informative. Correlations among the three kept models: GRU–LightGBM 0.983, TabPFN–LightGBM 0.990, TabPFN–GRU 0.985.

Ensembling and post-processing

A plain weighted average of the three probabilities — no meta-model, no per-customer switching. Both of those were tested and were among the worst results recorded; a feature-aware meta-model came in 0.00208 worse than simple averaging.

How the weights were chosen

Grid search here means exactly what it sounds like: try every combination and keep the best. Nothing is being fitted — three numbers are being looked up.

  • Collect three prediction vectorsEach model produced a probability for all 24,000 training customers — crucially, out-of-fold, where every customer was scored by a model that never saw their label. Those numbers are honest stand-ins for unseen data.
  • Enumerate the possibilitiesStep each weight in increments of 0.05, keeping only combinations summing to 1: about 230 valid triples.
  • Score each oneFor a candidate like (0.5, 0.3, 0.2), compute the blended probability for all 24,000 customers and measure log loss against the true outcomes. One number per candidate.
  • Keep the lowest(0.45, 0.30, 0.25) scored 0.42136, the best of the 230.

The flatness is the reassuring part. Every weighting from 0.50 to 0.70 on LightGBM lands within 0.0001, so the optimum is a broad plateau rather than a sharp peak. A sharp peak would suggest the weights had latched onto noise in these particular 24,000 rows; a plateau means almost any sensible weighting performs the same. We tested that directly: shrinking the weights halfway toward equal — the standard remedy for weights overfitted to validation data — made the hidden-test score worse (0.41045 against 0.41032, measured at the two-model stage). This is the one step in the pipeline where genuine selection happens, so it is where overfitting risk lives; the flat surface and the failed shrinkage test are the evidence that the risk did not materialise.

For a customer where LightGBM says 30%, the GRU says 20% and TabPFN says 25%, the submitted probability is 0.45×0.30 + 0.30×0.20 + 0.25×0.25 = 26%. The averaging is mathematically safe for this metric: log loss is convex, so by Jensen’s inequality the loss of an averaged prediction never exceeds the average of the individual losses.

Post-processing: one clip, and nothing else

No calibration is applied, and that is a finding rather than an omission. Four methods were tested, each under nested cross-validation against a 0.42136 baseline.

The only post-processing is clipping the final probabilities to [1e-4, 1−1e-4], insurance against a single confidently-wrong prediction contributing an enormous penalty.

Three observations that generalise

  • New information wins; new algorithms do notSeven model families were rejected outright, while one accounting identity produced our largest gain. Every gain we recorded came either from deriving a quantity the data did not state, or from averaging more of the same models — never from a more sophisticated algorithm.
  • Cross-validation understated the feature gain and overstated the averaging gainThe spending features measured +0.00046 on OOF and delivered −0.00173 on the hidden test — 3.6× larger. Repeated CV measured a gain on OOF and delivered nothing. The direction of the error is systematic, not random: OOF cannot see anything that changes how many models vote on a test row.
  • Decorrelation is necessary but not sufficientOur most decorrelated model earned weight 0.00; the GRU earned 0.30 at a higher correlation. Disagreement only helps when it is informative.

Strengths

  • Three genuinely different inductive biasesAggregate statistics, temporal sequence, and a pretrained prior. Diversity is structural rather than cosmetic — models that merely differed in algorithm all received weight zero.
  • Calibrated without post-processingProbabilities can be read as probabilities. Four independent calibration tests each concluded no adjustment was warranted, which matters for a lender setting its own risk thresholds.
  • Low variance by construction270 cross-validated fits across six fold partitions, plus 11 full-data refits, stand behind each prediction. Averaging involves no parameter selection, so it cannot overfit — the one component of the design with a mathematical guarantee.
  • Interpretable where it countsThe 45%-weight component is a tree model with SHAP attributions available per customer, so any individual decision can be explained by feature contribution.
  • No fragile tricksNo pseudo-labelling, no rank transforms, no leaderboard-fitted post-processing. Every step was validated out-of-fold before adoption.

Limitations

  • Part of the target is unreachable from this dataThe twenty worst-predicted customers are all defaulters the model rated at 2–4% risk, every one with a spotless payment record. Whatever drove those defaults is absent from the columns we were given.
  • One subgroup is served noticeably worseOur fairness audit found EDUCATION="other" (387 customers, 28 defaults) is over-predicted by 61% relative and ranked poorly (AUC 0.645 against ~0.79 elsewhere). That category is where our own cleaning collapses undocumented codes 0, 5 and 6, so it is a heterogeneous catch-all — and the only group where the model does worse than simply predicting that group’s base rate.
  • Most of the value sits in one modelLightGBM alone reaches 0.42211 out-of-fold; all three together reach 0.42136. The other two components buy roughly 0.0007 for 180 extra fits and two additional dependencies.
  • Blend weights are fitted to validation dataUnlike the averaging, weight selection is genuine selection and carries overfitting risk. Mitigated but not eliminated, as described in §6.
  • TabPFN is a reproducibility liabilityIt needs a Prior Labs licence, an API token and a weights download, and takes ~50 minutes to run — a real cost for 0.25 of the blend.

What we would try next, and why we stopped

Work stopped at the deadline, not at the point where ideas ran out. These are the specific things left on the table, recorded so the stopping point is a documented decision rather than an unexplained gap.

1  TabPFN internal ensembling

TabPFN ran with n_estimators=4 — the number of times it re-reads the data under different feature orderings before averaging. Raising it to 8 or 16 improves the quality of each fit, which is different from the repeated cross-validation we already exhausted (that averages more fits of the same quality). It is the only genuinely untested knob left on the winning configuration.

Why not done: the run at n_estimators=4 took 3h 10m, and under 4 hours remained before the deadline with no way to submit a partial result.

An avoidable cost: TabPFN was run on CPU. It was set that way on the first pilot to sidestep a possible incompatibility with Apple’s Metal backend, and never revisited. Tested afterwards, device="mps" works correctly and is faster even on a small subset (4.8s against 6.8s on 3,000 rows, identical predictions to float precision). The full run would plausibly have taken about an hour rather than three — which would have made this experiment affordable. The constraint was a wrong assumption, not the hardware.

2  A GRU trained on more channels

The sequence model reads 8 channels per month. The spending features that produced our largest gain reach it only as flattened static inputs, never as an ordered per-month trajectory. We tested exactly this and measured +0.00002 — but at 3 partitions rather than 6, and with the spending features already present as statics in both arms, so the test isolated only whether ordering helps. A cleaner design would compare against a GRU with no spending information at all.

Why not done: the measured effect was inside noise, and the cleaner experiment needs two full GRU runs at roughly 50 minutes each.

3  More TabPFN fold partitions

TabPFN carries 25% of the blend on 30 fold-fits, against LightGBM’s 90 and the GRU’s 150. It remains the least-averaged component, so it sits earliest on the variance-reduction curve where each doubling buys proportionally more.

Why not done: going 3→6 partitions moved out-of-fold log loss by 0.00002. The leaderboard gained 0.00013, but that came mostly from the full-data refit added in the same run — which now exists and cannot be added twice.

The honest position at the deadline. None of the remaining ideas is worth more than roughly 0.0001 on the evidence available, and each costs hours. That is not a claim the model cannot be improved — the leader demonstrably has more — but the levers we can still name are exhausted, and the ones we would reach for next are already measured as saturating. A better result would most likely require a genuinely new insight about the data rather than more of what is described here.

Appendix: how one prediction is actually computed

Everything above is the method. This is the mechanism — one real customer, late last month and the month before, carrying a balance close to their limit, followed through each of the three models. Skippable; nothing in sections 1–7 depends on it.

A.1  LightGBM: hundreds of small yes/no checklists, added together

A decision tree is a flowchart of yes/no questions ending in a verdict. This model builds hundreds of them, each deliberately short — four questions deep at most.

Where the questions come from

Nobody writes them. At each point in the tree the algorithm tries every feature at every sensible threshold — is PAY_0 above 0, above 1, above 2; is util_mean above 0.3, 0.4, 0.5 — and measures how much separating the customers that way would reduce its error. That measure is the gain. It keeps the single best split, then repeats the search on each side.

Below is one actual first tree from a trained model, not an illustration. One number stands out: its first split scores a gain of 2,254, while the best split anywhere below it scores 248 — nine times smaller.

An important caveat before reading it. This is one tree out of 90 models × hundreds of trees each; change the seed or the fold and the tree changes. We checked how much: across 30 models, the very first split was PAY_0 ≤ 1.5 in 13 of them, months_since_late in 12, and the recent_late split shown below in only 5. But notice what those competing splits all are — four ways of asking whether the customer was recently late. Every one of the 30 models opens on recent lateness; it simply reaches for whichever measure of it happened to be available, since feature_fraction=0.5 means each tree only sees half the features. The individual tree is unstable; the pattern it finds is not.

recent_late <= 0.75?  gain 2254   — were they late in the last two months?
├─ YES (mostly safe)  → payratio_min ≤ 0.0001?  gain 248
│   ├─ YES → bill_std ≤ 2736?      → … 4 leaves, values −1.24 to −1.27
│   └─ NO  → LIMIT_BAL ≤ 75,000?   → … 4 leaves, values −1.26 to −1.28

A real customer through the real model

Taking an actual customer from the validation fold: late two months running, using 100% of their limit, and having paid under 5% of a recent bill.

Two things to notice. The first tree barely moves the needle — a nudge of 0.06 — because each tree’s influence is deliberately shrunk by the learning rate (0.03). The work is done collectively: 352 trees each contributing a fraction. The second tree then trains on what the first got wrong, the third on what the first two still miss. That is what “boosting” means, and it is why no single tree needs to be clever. The model adds in log-odds rather than probabilities, because log-odds can be summed safely while probabilities cannot; one formula converts back at the end.

Reading trees responsibly. The customer above is traced through one real model and its arithmetic is exact. A different seed would route them through different questions and produce a slightly different number — which is precisely why the final prediction averages 90 of these rather than trusting any one. Individual trees are for understanding the mechanism; only the average is the model.

Why keep the trees so short? Four questions deep means a tree can combine at most four facts about someone. That sounds limiting, but with only 24,000 customers a deeper tree starts carving out branches describing a handful of specific individuals rather than a real pattern. We tested 135 configurations; every deeper or less-constrained one scored worse.

A.2  GRU: forming an impression while reading the file month by month

LightGBM sees each customer as one row of summary numbers, and those summaries throw away the order events happened in. The GRU is built to keep that order. Think of a loan officer reading a customer’s file one month at a time, oldest page first, forming an impression and revising it with each new page. That is close to literally what this model does.

What “an impression” means here

The model keeps a set of 32 numbers that it updates after every month. These aren’t anything a human named — they’re whatever the model found useful to track during training. One might end up representing something like “how recently things went wrong”, another “whether payments are shrinking”. Collectively they’re the model’s running notes on this customer.

What happens at each month

Each month supplies the 8 channels listed in §3. The model then makes two judgements, and this is the part that makes a GRU more than a running average:

  • How much should this month change my view?A month that looks like the last one barely moves the notes. A sudden missed payment moves them a lot.
  • How much of my earlier view still matters?If someone has clearly recovered, the model can let old trouble fade rather than holding it against them forever.

Both judgements are made by small formulas the model learned during training — nobody told it that recent months matter more, or that recovery should count. It worked that out from 24,000 customers’ outcomes. It then reads the six months forwards and again backwards, producing two sets of notes; reading backwards lets it interpret an early month with knowledge of how things turned out.

  month 6 (oldest)   notes ← blank, then updated by month 6
  month 5            notes revised
  month 4, 3, 2      notes revised
  month 1 (newest)   notes final    → 32 numbers forwards
  read backwards                    → 32 numbers backwards
  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  those 64 + the 81 summary features
  → two small layers of arithmetic  → 34.3%

The case for including it at all. Two customers were each late twice. One was late in months 5 and 6 and has paid cleanly since. The other paid cleanly until missing months 1 and 2. Their n_late, pay_max and pay_mean are exactly identical — LightGBM cannot tell them apart, because the summaries genuinely contain no difference. One is recovering and one is falling apart. Reading months in order separates them, and that is what earned this model 30% of the blend.

A.3  TabPFN: a model that was trained to learn, not trained on us

This one is unusual enough that “a pretrained model” undersells it. It never trains on our data — not one pass, not one adjustment to its internals. Two separate things have to happen for it to work, and only the first involves training.

Part one: what Prior Labs did, long before we showed up

They built a generator that invents fake datasets. Each starts with an invented cause-and-effect structure — imaginary variables influencing each other through randomly chosen relationships, with random noise thrown in — and produces a table of features and outcomes describing nothing real. They generated millions of these and trained a neural network on one repeated exercise: here are some rows with their answers, and one row without — what’s the answer? Then the dataset is thrown away and the next one is a completely different invented world.

Because no dataset ever appears twice, the network cannot memorise any of them. The only thing that helps it improve is getting better at the general skill: look at labelled examples, work out the pattern linking features to outcomes, apply it to a new row.

Part two: what happens when we use it

We hand it our 19,200 training customers together with their known outcomes, plus the one customer we want predicted — all in a single pass. It compares that customer against all 19,200 labelled examples, works out which ones they resemble in the ways that turned out to matter, and produces a probability.

  in:  19,200 customers + their known outcomes
     + 1 customer needing a prediction
       ↓ one pass through the network
  out: 31.2%

Nothing about the network changes in the process. Swap in different training customers and its answer changes immediately — because the training data is an input to it, the way a question is an input, rather than something it absorbs and keeps.

Why this helps on a dataset our size. LightGBM and the GRU begin knowing nothing and must learn everything about credit risk from our 24,000 customers. TabPFN arrives already holding sensible general expectations about how tabular data behaves. It has less to learn from our limited rows — which is why it matched our fully-tuned GRU with no tuning whatsoever, and why the mistakes it makes differ from models that learned everything here. Different mistakes are exactly what makes an ensemble worth having.

What it costs. The trained network is external and licence-gated, it needs the entire training set handed to it every time it predicts, and it slows down as that set grows — about six minutes per fold here, against seconds for LightGBM.

Ensemble: 0.45 × LightGBM (81 features, 90 fits + full-data refit) + 0.30 × bidirectional GRU (6×8 panel, 150 fits + refit) + 0.25 × TabPFN (30 fits + refit, pretrained).
